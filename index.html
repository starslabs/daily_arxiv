☆ ArchSpace: Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing LLM Architecture Research
Jie Wang, Tong Zhu, JiaHao Kuang, Zhe Xu, Yunkai Chai, Dehong Zheng, Yu Cheng, Tao Ji, ZhouHan Lin, Qi Zhang, Bowen Zhou
Model architecture shapes the scaling behavior, training efficiency, and inference cost of LLMs, making it a central focus of both industry and academia. In industry, teams such as those behind DeepSeek, Qwen, and Kimi routinely introduce architectural innovations, yet public reports rarely document which ideas succeeded, which were abandoned, and why. In academia, limited compute and inconsistent baselines often restrict evaluations to small-scale, single-point comparisons, making it difficult to establish whether improvements hold at larger scales or translate into broader impact. ArchSpace addresses both gaps as a public platform for Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing Architecture Researc}: each proposal is recorded with its experiments and reasoning, reviewed by the community, and referenced by later work. To evaluate ideas before costly large-scale training, ArchSpace leverages scaling laws to predict model loss at larger scales from smaller experiments, a practice widely adopted in industry. We provide initial references that make the platform usable: Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 baselines, fitted scaling laws, and a reusable training recipe. Ideas validated through this review can in turn become new baselines, and ArchSpace thereby aims to broaden the range of ideas that shape the next generation of models.
☆ Learning When Diffusion Language Models Should Regenerate
Minhae Oh, Jeonghye Kim, Jinwoo Koo, Yeongoon Kim, Jimin Seo, Minsik Seol, Jungwoo Lee
Remasking allows diffusion language models to revisit generated text, but effective correction requires knowing when and what to revise. Our diagnosis on Sudoku reveals two gaps. Hidden states contain error information that token confidence does not reliably expose, and remasking an isolated error usually reproduces it when the surrounding context remains fixed. These findings motivate DiVR , a verifier that predicts whether a partially generated sequence will reach a correct answer. Trained on the final correctness of the model's own trajectories, DiVR decides when to regenerate a block and which alternative to retain, while the generator itself stays unchanged. On five reasoning benchmarks with LLaDA-8B-Base, DiVR raises the average accuracy of the fine-tuned generator from 35.3\% to 43.4\% at $1.9$-$3.5\times$ the cost of greedy decoding, above search baselines that use $3.8$-$7.4\times$. It also complements post-training such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, and generalizes to other generators, including Dream.
☆ CoReMem: Cost-Internalizing Memory for Successive Coding Tasks with Prospective Valuation
Peiwen Li, Harshit Verma, Sophia Zhou, Haiwen Wang, Ngoc Bui, Heng Chang, Fei Fei, David van Dijk, Rex Ying
Coding agents tackle successive tasks on an evolving repository, carrying forward edits and writing memory before the next task is known. Selecting memory for unknown next tasks requires balancing future usefulness against the costs of writing and reading it. The actor must also decide when acting on available memory saves effort as the task unfolds. We introduce Coding-Repository Memory (CoReMem), a cost-internalizing framework that guides memory design and actor adaptation through measured memory value on the next task. Prospective segment valuation compares runs of the next task with and without individual trajectory segments from the same inherited repository state, guiding a compact memory built around anchors: repository locations and executable checks. Actor adaptation estimates on-policy memory value within rollout groups with and without memory, using a reward that prioritizes resolution and penalizes step count in successful runs. Provenance-gated value credit assigns the additional value term to steps identified as using memory-supplied anchors, while every step retains outcome credit. On ChainSWE, CoReMem raises the 27B actor's resolve rate from 21.0% to 25.7%, with 17% fewer steps and 11% fewer total cache-aware tokens, including memory-module calls, relative to the untrained actor model without memory.
☆ Linking Rapid Learning to Compositional Structure in Neural Networks: Evidence from Additive Analogies in Meta-Learned Parameter Spaces
Abigail L. Tenenbaum, R. Thomas McCoy
Humans can learn rapidly from limited experience, while neural networks typically require extensive training. In this work, we investigate what properties of a neural network can enable it to learn rapidly. We study neural networks that have been optimized via meta-learning, a technique that exposes a network to many related functions to facilitate its subsequent learning of similar functions. Prior work has shown that these meta-learned networks can learn new functions rapidly, but how they do so remains unclear. To investigate their internal structure, we take inspiration from classic work demonstrating additive relationships among the learned vectors that neural networks use to represent words (e.g., "king" minus "man" plus "woman" approximates "queen"). Analogously, we look for additive relationships among a meta-learned network's "representations" of different functions; when a meta-learned network rapidly learns to implement a specific function, all of its adapted parameter values can be concatenated into a single vector that we treat as representing the function. We find that such parameter vectors can be added and subtracted according to symbolic relationships among the functions they represent, yielding new parameter vectors that successfully represent the functions predicted by the corresponding symbolic manipulations: the networks specified by these artificially recombined vectors actually implement the intended functions without any direct training on them. Our results suggest that meta-learning can tune a network's starting point to organize subsequent learning along systematic, functionally meaningful trajectories through the space of possible parameter vectors, providing a geometric basis for realizing inductive biases in neural networks.

☆ Goal Encoding in LLMs
Arnab Sen Sharma, Eric Todd, Sheridan Feucht, Andy Arditi, Rohit Gandikota, Natalie Shapira, Gabriele Sarti, Constanza Fierro, Lisa Schut, David Bau
We investigate whether and how LLMs encode goals: internal neural representations that precede and guide action, and that are flexible enough to be realized through many different execution paths. We construct a coding setting with multiple sub-tasks that allows us to evaluate goal-seeking behavior and the selection of sub-goals. In this setting, we localize an encoding of the next sub-goal in the outputs of a sparse set of attention heads. The same heads can be used to decode the LM's future actions in natural language (e.g., "I'm checking if a word is a palindrome"). We verify that this encoding is causal for next action and is plastic: transplanting it into a different environment, e.g., another programming language, triggers the same operation realized with a different sequence of tokens. The LM also sustains the encoding throughout the action's execution, which is puzzling because a transformer LM without recurrence cannot carry a state forward for more tokens than it has layers left. We propose that it does not need to. Under a non-recurrent goal-seeking (NRGS) account, the LM frequently re-reads the goal from the task specification, compares it with the progress visible in the growing context, and pursues what remains. We test the predictions of this account with targeted causal interventions.

☆ Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards NeurIPS 2026
Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang, Chaojie Wang, Bo An
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) typically relies on a verifier that can reliably judge whether sampled solutions are correct. However, in many realistic settings, complete verification is generally unavailable, while cheap checks can only falsify failures. We formalize this regime as one-sided verifiability (OSV): verified negatives are reliable counterexamples, but verified positives are merely non-falsified candidates and may contain many false positives. This asymmetry makes standard RLVR unreliable, because reinforcing verified positives can directly reward shortcuts that pass partial checks. To address this issue, we propose Negative-Only Policy Optimization (NOPO), which treats verified positives as unlabeled and updates the policy only by suppressing trusted verified negatives. Specifically, NOPO combines a reference-relative negative suppression loss with a group-level adapter that scales each update by the proportion of non-falsified responses. Across unit-test, constraint-check, and LLM self-verifiers, NOPO improves pass@k over baselines on code, logic, and math benchmarks when oracle rewards are unavailable. These gains persist under inference-time scaling and cross-benchmark transfer.

Gautam Galada, Supreeth Shastri, Sagnik Ray Choudhury
We propose Synthesis Smuggling, a novel threat model in which an adversarial LLM infers sensitive information about a user from routine assistance-seeking interactions and steganographically encodes that into innocuous-looking model-generated content. The user may then unknowingly transmit that information by sharing the generated artifact, say as an email, social-media post, or message, through ordinary communication channels. ☆ Maximizing Capacity Utilization of LoRA Adapters for Mitigating the Learning-Forgetting Trade-off
Tomohiro Sawada, Kartik Goyal
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) often underperforms full fine-tuning, especially in heterogeneous and continual-learning settings in which substantial forgetting occurs. In general, it suffers from an undesirable learning-forgetting trade-off across diverse fine-tuning settings. We identify a potential contributor: LoRA adapters, especially high-rank ones, frequently fail to realize the available capacity afforded by the configured rank. Specifically, we find that standard LoRA updates have effective rank far below their configured rank, limiting how broadly the update energy is distributed. Therefore, we propose training mechanisms that optimize the original objective while maximizing adapter effective rank. Our main method adds a simple, stable, differentiable, and SVD-free Rényi-2 effective-rank regularizer to the language-modeling loss. We also connect capacity maximization to norm-bounded updates. In mixed-domain rank sweeps on three models evaluated with multiple random seeds, a penalty weight of $λ=0.01$ lowers mean Wiki perplexity at every tested rank of at least $16$ relative to unpenalized LoRA. Mean training perplexity improves or increases by at most $0.02$ in all but one model-rank comparison. Single-task and scheduling experiments further show that the balance between learning and retention depends on the configuration. We also observe that several LoRA variants in the literature also suffer from the identified capacity issue, and our approach provides a complementary axis of improvement to LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning. These results suggest that effective rank is not only a post-hoc diagnostic of adapter quality, but a trainable objective for improving the learning-forgetting frontier. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. ☆ CapLite: Prompt-Injection Defense for Small Language Model Agents without Model-Written Security Artifacts
Zimo Xu, Wenzhuo Liu, Hideki Nakayama
Enforcement defenses against prompt injection, such as CaMeL, Progent, IPIGuard and DRIFT, ask the agent's own model to write a security artifact (a program, a privilege policy, a dependency graph or a constraint list) that code then enforces. We ask whether small language model (SLM) agents can be trusted to write these artifacts. Across 15 open-weight models, 13 of them between 1.7B and 12B parameters, they cannot: the policies, graphs and constraints that the agent's model writes admit attacks on 14 (Progent), 13 (IPIGuard) and 11 (DRIFT) of the models, and CaMeL's programs fail to run in up to 68% of episodes. Arnab Sanyal, Gourav Datta, Sandeep P. Chinchali

☆ Optimal Single-History Tokenization Disambiguation for Provably Secure Linguistic Steganography
Feng Jiang
Provably secure linguistic steganography assumes that the receiver recovers the sender's tokens, but a text channel delivers only text, and ambiguous tokenizations break extraction. Existing fixes change the distribution, synchronize whole pools of tokens at a capacity that collapses with large vocabularies, or track several token histories. We prove that if extraction is exact and error-free across full-support language models, a single-history receiver is confined to a prefix-free candidate set at every step -- even if it reads the entire future text and the sender constrains later steps -- and must therefore give up the stop entropy, the entropy of deciding whether each prefix token ends there. Fixing these stop decisions on the prefix forest with shared randomness yields coin projection: a closed-form scheme that attains the bound, works with any distribution coder in one model call per token, preserves ordinary sampling, and refines SyncPool for every key. Across English, Chinese and Japanese, ambiguous text is rare (at most 11 in 1,000 tokens), yet the stop entropy at word and morpheme prefixes takes 7.2-9.0% of the entropy at every step; SyncPool's further loss comes from space and single-byte UTF-8 hubs that merge subtrees while carrying almost no stop uncertainty. Coin projection keeps 91-93% of the string entropy for the coder at every vocabulary size; with the full vocabulary, it turns 2.16-6.85 times as much entropy into message bits as SyncPool and recovers every completed message.

♻ ☆ Examining the Impact of Data and Training Methods on Emergent and Subliminal Misalignment NeurIPS 2026
