☆ Tokenisation is not in APX
Violeta Kastreva, Philip Whittington, Dennis Komm, Tiago Pimentel
In this paper, we prove that, unless P=NP, no polynomial-time algorithm can find a tokeniser whose objective is within any constant factor of the optimum, meaning that tokenisation is not in the APX complexity class. We prove this for direct and bottom-up tokenisation, under both a compression or unigram log-likelihood objectives, and for binary or larger alphabets. We further show that, even over unary alphabets, direct tokenisation is APX-hard, and thus admits no polynomial-time approximation scheme.

☆ Diverse Alternative Reasoning Traces for Hard Math Problems NeurIPS 2026
Lukas Aichberger, Adam Golinski, Arno Blaas, Arwen Bradley, Jacob Springer, Mehrdad Farajtabar, Michael Kirchhof, Sinead Williamson
Post-training large language models (LLMs) for mathematical reasoning often relies on accepted traces whose final answers pass a verifier. For hard math problems, however, such traces can be rare under independent Monte Carlo (MC) sampling. To address this, we propose Diverse Alternative Reasoning Traces (DART), a trace-mining method that starts from a rejected trace, decomposes it into reasoning steps, and ranks them by position-weighted uncertainty. It then generates deliberately distinct alternative candidates for highly ranked steps using reference-answer guidance and a meta-thought before completing the resulting traces. On greedy-unsolved DeepMath-103K problems, DART recovers 37% more problems than MC sampling at a matched number of generated traces. Its estimated generation cost per recovered problem is nearly identical, while its cost per accepted trace is 30% lower. Post-training Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on traces mined by DART achieves the highest point estimate in five of six evaluations across MATH-500 and AIME benchmarks. These results demonstrate that revisiting rejected traces with DART can expand problem coverage and provide effective post-training data.

☆ Mixture-of-Loops Transformer
Lianghui Zhu, Shijie Wang, Xinggang Wang, Jinze Bai, Bo Wang, Xiang Bai
Looped Transformers boost expressivity by reusing shared layers across loops, yet at the cost of efficiency. Pushing their expressivity-efficiency frontier requires rethinking the core mechanism connecting adjacent loops: What should be passed as a Transformer loops? We introduce Mixture-of-Loops Transformer (MoLT), whose Mixture-of-Loops Attention (MoLA) uses keys and values (KV) as memory for selective reuse across depths and loops. Under loop residual, MoLT-R retains the residual stream and reads local KV across loops for expressivity. Under loop KV, MoLT-KV instead restarts from embeddings and reads current-loop local KV with preceding-loop memory KV at earlier positions for efficiency. MoLT-KV eliminates same-position cross-loop dependence and, paired with loop forcing for parallel training, unlocks single-traversal Mixture-of-Loops (MoL) decoding beyond the training loop horizon. To balance efficiency and quality, we introduce U-shaped MoLT (U-MoLT), which separates memory construction from prediction readout and skips unused decoder traversals without changing predictions. In 400B-token pretraining, U-MoLT outperforms OLMo3 across 700M/1.5B dense and 3B MoE models, achieving 1.7x validation cross-entropy (CE) token efficiency at 700M while improving 3B MoE 10-domain perplexity by 0.75 and 10-task accuracy by 2.1 percentage points. In 100B-token continued pretraining of Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, our U-MoLT adaptation lowers CE on all 11 domains over the matched baseline with 1.4x mean token efficiency. At inference, U-MoLT with MoL-Hybrid-2 matches non-looped OLMo3 latency at 996.6 vs. 1,000.6 ms on S-size (~113M) models. Code is available at https://github.com/hustvl/MoLT

☆ Proverbs As A Window On Collective Prescriptions
Tomás Lejarraga, Dirk U. Wulff, Ralph Hertwig
Proverbs, as repositories of traditional wisdom, have informed human reasoning for millennia. Yet the collective prescriptions they encode have remained largely unquantified. We introduce a computational framework to analyze nearly 50,000 English-language proverbs. Using large language models, validated against human judgments, we map the topical landscape of proverbs, assess their prescriptive power, and quantify their recommendations on key dimensions of human judgment and behavior. We find that most proverbs are prescriptive, offering guidance in domains under individual control, such as preparation and prudent action, whereas proverbs about uncontrollable circumstances, such as fate and mortality, tend to describe rather than prescribe. When prescriptive proverbs take a consistent stance, they favor caution over risk, long-term over short-term outcomes, honesty over deception, and kindness over malice, and show a moderate tendency toward suspicion over trust. On dimensions involving genuine trade-offs, especially autonomy versus compliance, proverbs provide contradicting advice rather than a single recommendation. This analysis provides a quantitative map of the collective prescriptions in a culture's proverbial wisdom, revealing a core of prudent, honest, and forward-looking guidance alongside varied advice where circumstances dictate the best course of action.
☆ Repairing the Trustworthiness of Distilled Language Models
Kumar Shubham, Devendra Vyas, Vidushi Agarwal, Jayin Khanna, Prathosh AP
Knowledge distillation enables the deployment of large language models through smaller student models, but the compression process can introduce regressions in trustworthiness even when downstream performance is preserved. Repairing such failures is challenging because the original distillation corpus is often unavailable, while direct preference optimization may alter capabilities inherited from the teacher. We propose a targeted repair framework based on the proximal Bregman response function (PBRF) that addresses both limitations. We estimate the curvature required for PBRF using a surrogate proxy dataset, removing dependence on the original transfer corpus, and extend the framework to preference-based supervision through an odds-ratio objective. This enables the use of preferred and dispreferred response pairs while constraining changes to the distilled model. Our results show that the proposed approach improves targeted trustworthiness behaviors for both pre-training and post-training models while largely preserving downstream performance, providing a practical alternative to re-distillation or unconstrained preference optimization. Our experiment results show that across different distilled models, the trustworthy regression can be improved by up to 8% with less than 1% decrement in perplexity.

A common remedy supplies complete solutions from a stronger model, but full trajectories are expensive and expose the learner to long off-policy histories. We introduce SPLICE, which constructs local training targets from failed current-policy trajectories online. For each all-fail group, a proposal model selects a splice point in a current-policy trajectory and generates several bounded options from that point. ☆ DFly2: Turbocharging Long-Context Speculative Decoding by Sharing the Target's Sparsity
Hong Liu, Rui Cen, Junhan Shi, Lin Niu, Runzhi Fan, Zhaojing Zhou, Haodi Lei, Jiebin Zhang, Saiyong Yang, Junqiang Zheng, Zhenghai Xue, Guanghua Yu, Jianchen Zhu
To avoid the quadratic cost of dense attention over long contexts, models such as DeepSeek-V4 and Hy4 have turned natively sparse, and speculative decoding, which matters most where decoding is slowest, increasingly runs on them. Their drafters, however, still keep a context cache of their own, over the whole prompt or a sliding window. Beyond memory, such a cache costs prefill time, prefix-cache hits and decode concurrency. Drafters that read the target's cache avoid one of their own, but settle only which cache, not which positions, and still read everything or a recent window. Neither rule is made for the query at hand, and computing a selection per query in the drafter would cost more as the context grows. Yet a natively sparse target already picks positions for each query, over a cache it keeps anyway. Both of the drafter's choices are therefore already made, and paid for, by the target: its cache and its selection. We measure how far one selection carries: made a step early, it still covers close to nine tenths of what each later token selects on DS-V4.1-Flash. We present DFly2, a block-parallel drafter that shares the target's sparsity. Each draft layer reads the KV its paired target layer cached (KV sharing), at the positions that layer's indexer selected, a fixed number anywhere in the context (Index sharing). DFly2 thus runs no scoring pass and keeps no cache of its own. On DS-V4.1-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash and Hy4~flash, whose sparse attention and architecture differ, it accepts more tokens per step than DFlash and DSpark trained on the same recipe, over general, long-context and agentic benchmarks. Its margin over the stronger baseline widens from 3.9%-10.5% on general workloads to 10.0%-31.5% on long-context and agentic ones. At high concurrency on DS-V4.1-Flash, at matched per-user decode speed, its throughput is 62% and 81% higher on two 64K sets.

☆ When the Proxy Is Wrong: Mismatch-Corrected Bandits for Adaptive Data Sampling
Amine Saadi, Elissa Mhanna, Raphaël Féraud, Gwénolé Lecorvé

However, this proxy can rank data differently from the target, leading to suboptimal training performance. To address this problem, we model the mismatch between the proxy and target models and incorporate it into the allocation decision. To do so, the problem is cast as a multi-fidelity bandit, where each arm is a data intervention. The proxy reward is observed every round, whereas the target reward is revealed only on rare audits of the same update. Experimentally, we test the approach in a closed-loop proof of concept on CivilComments-WILDS, where BERT-Small guides the training of BERT-Large. Mismatch correction improves worst-group accuracy over a target-only policy with the same target-model budget. Spreading audits over training improves worst-group accuracy by 6.3 to 10.5 percentage points at the same audit count.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) has become the de facto architecture for hundred-billion-parameter language models, yet its advantages at sub-billion scales for on-device deployment remain largely unexplored. To close this gap, we present MobileMoE, a family of on-device MoE language models with sub-billion active parameters (0.3-0.9B active and 1.3-5.3B total) that establish a new Pareto frontier for on-device LLMs. We first formulate an on-device MoE scaling law that jointly optimizes MoE architecture under mobile memory and compute constraints, identifying an on-device sweet spot - moderate sparsity with fine-grained and shared experts - that is simultaneously memory and compute-optimal. Building on the derived architectures, we train MobileMoE with a four-stage recipe covering pre-training, mid-training, instruction fine-tuning, and quantization-aware training, all on open-source datasets. Across 14 benchmarks, MobileMoE matches or exceeds leading on-device dense LLMs with 2-4$\times$ fewer inference FLOPs, and matches or surpasses the state-of-the-art MoE OLMoE-1B-7B with up to 60% fewer parameters. To bridge the last mile to mobile deployment, we provide the first efficient MoE inference on commodity smartphones with comprehensive on-device profiling. At comparable INT4 weight memory, MobileMoE-S delivers $1.8$-$3.8\times$ faster prefill and $2.2$-$3.4\times$ faster decode than the dense baseline MobileLLM-Pro.

♻ ☆ Speak or Stay Silent: Context-Aware Turn-Taking in Multi-Party Dialogue
Kratika Bhagtani, Mrinal Anand, Yu Chen, Xu, Amit Kumar Singh Yadav
Existing voice AI assistants treat every detected pause as an invitation to speak. This works in dyadic dialogue, but in multi-party settings, where an AI assistant participates alongside multiple speakers, pauses are abundant and ambiguous. An assistant that speaks on every pause becomes disruptive rather than useful. In this work, we formulate context-aware turn-taking: at every detected pause, given the full conversation context, our method decides whether the assistant should speak or stay silent. We introduce a benchmark of over 120K labeled conversations spanning three multi-party corpora. Evaluating eight recent large language models, we find that they consistently fail at context-aware turn-taking under zero-shot prompting. We then propose a supervised fine-tuning approach with reasoning traces, improving balanced accuracy by up to 23 percentage points. Our findings suggest that context-aware turn-taking is not an emergent capability; it must be explicitly trained.
